Extending Instagram for Human Artists

Introducing a community notes feature dedicated to identifying deceptive AI-generated art to others, sourced from the community for the community.

01

Overview

Currently, floods of AI-generated slop on social media platforms like Instagram threaten hobbyists and aspiring artists.

At first glance, doesn't this image look well-drawn? Look a bit closer and you might notice her missing finger and other illogical inconsistencies. Too many times I've been deceived by AI-generated slop on social media. Generative AI has improved to the point it's difficult to tell what's AI-generated or not. But when there's no transparency online about what's created by AI versus humans, an image drawn line-by-line by human hands is unjustly placed on the same playing field as one created with a prompt and click.

The issue is that AI-generated posts are often deceptive and lack transparency about their gen-AI usage.

This takes recognition, engagement, and potential business opportunities away from real human artists. In addition, AI's deceptive presence has caused much distrust towards online art as a whole, causing real artists to face scrutiny and accusations of creating inauthentic artwork.

  • AI-generated art has been deceptively passed off as human-made, stealing commission work and thousands of engagements that should have gone to real artists.
  • Prominent artists like Karla Ortiz and Sarah Anderson cite AI as a direct cause of lost work — detailed in a PBS article.
  • AI models are trained on millions of human-made artworks without creator consent, raising serious ethical concerns.
  • Deceptive AI content has lowered people's trust in online art — in one viral instance on X, a fanartist was falsely accused of using AI and bullied off the platform entirely.
  • Artists will leave platforms that don't protect their work — DeviantArt saw mass account deletions after automatically opting artwork into AI training models and a new upcoming anti-AI app Cara gained over 600,000 users in a single week after Meta announced all content could be used as training data.

A platform becoming flooded with unlabeled "slop" could also drastically lower people's impression of the company's brand overall. In our user research, all our interviewees expressed a strong desire for AI-generated visual content on social media to be identified. And according to a 2025 survey by the Pew Research Center:

76%
of Americans think it's "extremely or very important" for AI-generated content to be labeled as such
53%
of Americans feel "not too or not at all confident" in their ability to spot AI-generated content

2025 Pew Research Center survey

So we designed a community-sourced feature for users to clearly identify AI-generated visual content to others.

We specifically focused on Instagram because it's image-based and currently doesn't have any effective solution. Our goal isn't to remove or ban AI-generated content, but to help the platform be more transparent about generative-AI to better serve human artists and the platform as a whole.

This is a complex issue; no single feature or redesign can completely solve it. However, implementing at least some infrastructure on the platform that helps address it would signal that the platform values creator's consent and human-made work, maintaining trust and reputation among artists and general users alike.

02

Preliminary User Research

We conducted user research to gain insight into personal experiences and perspectives on generative AI in the online art space. Our interviewees consisted of three hobbyist artists (people who share their art online for fun or as a side hustle but have no desire to pursue it professionally), one aspiring professional artist, and one non-artist who enjoys viewing artwork on social media. Notably, we made sure to interview at least one non-artist because the potential users of our extension are not just artists, but anyone who cares about the issue.

Despite coming from a variety of backgrounds, they all expressed a strongly negative stance against AI-generated "art." Their reasons varied, however, from its lack of creativity and/or quality, to the training models scraping real artwork without permission, to its threat to the careers of real human artists.

Seeing AI have such a huge presence in the professional world feels like "salt in the wound" because it was trained on the work of the very artists they stole from. — an Instagram illustrator interviewee

Although all interviewees expressed frustration over AI art showing up on their feed, they also all described being somewhat desensitized to it. Nowadays, most simply just scroll past or block AI content accounts without thinking much about it.

"I don't care about it anymore. Don't want to give it any engagement so I just wanna scroll past it." — non-artist interviewee

Because they've seen it so often before but can't actively do anything about it, all they can do is avoid giving it further engagement.

When asked about what they wished social media platforms would do about the flood of AI-generated content, surprisingly all of the interviewees unanimously wanted that all AI content would be identified in some form. But this isn't easy. Auto-detectors are unreliable. False accusations on other platforms like X are at times rampant. As one of our interviewees had unfortunately experienced herself, people who publicly call out content as AI-generated online can face harassment. We had to take all these considerations into our design.

Our interviews weren't just for user research purposes; we also got to know and understand our interviewees as artists and as people. We had the opportunity to see some of their personal workspaces and have them walk us through their creation processes. We heard about why they started drawing and what they liked drawing. We got to see first-hand how important art is to their everyday lives, which reaffirmed the importance of the purpose behind our design project.

03

Initial Prototypes

User Flow #1: Reporting Add-on and New AI Tag

This user flow enables users to report misleading AI content using the existing report feature on Instagram. When enough of these reports have been filed against a post, a new AI tag appears on the post to inform users. This was designed to address users' need for an option to specifically report content that does not explicitly disclose AI-use in its creation.

Entrypoint: We added a new "Misleading AI-generated content" option to the existing report page of Instagram. During our user interviews, the interviewee expressed frustration that there currently isn't any way to report deceptive AI-generated artwork on the platform. The best they could do was the existing "I just don't like it" option—which felt ineffective and unsatisfying.

When the user taps on the new "Misleading AI-generated content" option, this is the new screen they are brought to. Here, the user is able to pinch the image to highlight one exemplary problem area, and can select all that apply from a list of multiple choice reasons. If an option isn't listed, they can write their own under 'Other.'

When enough user reports have been submitted, a non-intrusive circular AI label appears on the top left of the post's image. When the users taps onto this label, this AI-info modal pops up from the bottom. It provides some basic statistics, such as the total number of current reports and the most commonly cited justification. We also included a way for people to affirm the AI label by tapping on the hyperlinked "Help report it here." which will take them to the New Misleading AI-generated Content Report Page.

User Flow #2: Community Notes

This user flow is enables users to write community notes to indicate to others a post is AI-generated. This was designed to address users' need for an option to comprehensively explain to others why they think a post is AI-generated. Currently, the only way to do this on is to write a comment—which isn't ideal as it boosts the engagement of the post, lacks visibility, and isn't anonymous. This feature was heavily inspired and referenced by X's community note feature. Note that in the time we were doing this project, Instagram introduced a community notes feature, but currently it is waitlist-only and not easily accessible.

Entrypoint: on the "More options" modal of each post, the user can tap on the community note option. This will then bring up a secondary modal that allows them to write, request, or rate a community note.

Writing a community note has three required components: an image markup feature, a confidence scale, and a text input box with the option of media uploads. Tapping on the image brings up another screen where users can markup the image with circles. The confidence scale allows users to express their certainty of the accusation, and the textbox allows for a written explanation. This feature affords a much more comprehensive review than the report feature, but is obviously much more intensive.

This is the screen where users can markup that highlight AI-generated inconsistencies and details with circles. They can add circles, delete them, and undo/redo. We also designed an alternative screen (not shown) with a free draw feature instead of the circle shape tool.

A community note on the post appears after enough users have rated it as helpful. While more visible than the circular AI label, it is also more intrusive. Tapping on "See full note" brings the user to a new screen—where they can view the full written note and see the circled suspected areas of the image. Additionally, users can see the % of people who've rated it as helpful, and contribute to this rating by upvote/downvoting it themselves.

04

User Testing

We interviewed four out of the same five users we spoke to during our preliminary user research and asked them what they thought about our intitial prototypes. Two preferred the reporting feature primarily for its convenience and ease, while the other two preferred the community notes for its flexibility and comprehensiveness. However, the two users who preferred the report feature admitted that they probably wouldn't actually use it; they simply preferred it because it was easier.

The greatest insight from these interviews was that our target user base isn't everyone.

The majority of people aren't willing to invest their time and energy into identifying AI art to others on the platform, but our target user base consists of the small minority who are—so our design should cater to them.

Another interesting finding: even though some participants didn't prefer the community notes, all four said they'd read the full community note and use the upvote/downvote buttons. So even if not everyone would write a community note themselves, they'd still engage with the feature. The community note feature only really needs one person to write it, while the report feature needs multiple users to submit a report—which may just be unrealistic. Furthermore, the existing reporting feature on Instagram is for purpose of taking down posts, not adding a label to them, so it felt misaligned.

So we decided that the community note feature was better suited for our purpose than the reporting + label feature.

From our interviews, we also received insightful feedback on various aspects of our community note feature. Here are the main highlights:

Surprisingly, all of our users didn't think the upload media feature was necessary since the post's image would already be included in the note. In fact, one user mentioned that it'd be inconvenient and disruptive to have to leave the current page or even the app to get outside screenshots or screen recordings.

Three out of four users preferred circle markup better than the freehand draw due to its simplicity and sufficiency. Also, one interviewee suggested the circles being automatically numbered so that you could more easily refer to a specific circle when writing the note. All users agreed there should be more than just one color option for the circles. However, they found the trash icon's function unintuitive: does it clear all circles or just one?

The confidence scale had mixed reception. One interviewee claimed it was unnecessary because "people going out of their way to actually write a community note" would always high confidence anyways. Contrary to this opinion, two users liked it because, with its nuance, they felt they could express their opinion even if they weren't entirely confident. However, a user expressed that it is unclear how the confidence scale factors into their community note.

05

Revised Prototypes

We then listened to our users and made the following changes to our prototypes:

Repurposing the existing "Drag to delete" UI from Instagram Stories Create Mode, we eliminated ambiguity about how to delete circles. Then, to make the writing process for our users easier, we implemented the circle numbering system one of our interviewees had suggested; it's now a lot simpler to specify which circled area you're referring to. We also added the option to change colors/line weights for circles; if the image was primarily red, for instance, having the option to choose big blue circles would help visibility.

On the markup screen, we simplified the white corner edges to be a thin white outline on the image to reduce confusion about its function. We opted for keeping the confidence scale because some of our interviewees felt the feature was important, especially for when they wanted to submit a note but when unconfident. We also removed the media upload feature our users found unnecessary, making this screen less cluttered and more purposeful.

Finally, on the community note, we added a caption under the title indicating the community note author's confidence that reflects the author's selection on the confidence scale. This is crucial because it adds nuance to the community note: if a note said low confidence, then it encourages the viewer to decide for themselves and take the AI accusation with a grain of salt. We also added a reporting feature for the sake of community safety: if a note is clearly malicious or inappropriate, reporting it would be better suited than simply giving it a thumbs down.
